Coral Reef Ecosystems: Structure and Importance

Coral reefs are among the most biodiverse ecosystems on Earth, often called the "rainforests of the sea." Despite covering less than 1% of the ocean floor, they support approximately 25% of all marine species.

Reef Structure:
Coral reefs are built by colonies of tiny animals called polyps, which secrete calcium carbonate to form hard skeletons. Over time, these skeletons accumulate into the reef structure. There are three main types of reefs:
1. Fringing reefs - grow directly from shorelines
2. Barrier reefs - separated from land by a lagoon (e.g., the Great Barrier Reef)
3. Atolls - ring-shaped reefs surrounding a central lagoon, typically formed around sunken volcanic islands

Ecological Importance:
- Provide habitat and breeding grounds for thousands of fish species
- Protect coastlines from storm surge and erosion by absorbing wave energy
- Support fisheries that feed hundreds of millions of people worldwide
- Contribute to nutrient cycling in otherwise nutrient-poor tropical waters

Economic Value:
Coral reefs generate an estimated $375 billion annually through tourism, fisheries, and coastal protection services. Countries like Australia, Indonesia, and the Philippines depend heavily on reef tourism.

Threats Beyond Bleaching:
- Overfishing and destructive fishing practices (dynamite fishing, cyanide fishing)
- Coastal development and habitat destruction
- Ocean acidification weakening coral skeletons
- Invasive species such as the crown-of-thorns starfish
- Plastic pollution and marine debris

Current Status: An estimated 50% of the world's coral reefs have been lost since the 1950s, with projections suggesting up to 90% could be lost by 2050 without significant climate action.
