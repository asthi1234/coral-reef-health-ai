Climate Change Impacts on Coral Reefs

Climate change is the single greatest long-term threat to coral reef survival worldwide, affecting reefs through multiple interconnected mechanisms.

Rising Ocean Temperatures:
Global sea surface temperatures have risen approximately 0.9 degrees Celsius since pre-industrial times. Even small temperature increases can push corals beyond their thermal tolerance, as most reef-building corals live very close to their upper temperature limits. Marine heatwaves, which are becoming more frequent and intense, are now the leading cause of mass bleaching events.

Ocean Acidification:
Oceans have absorbed roughly 30% of human-produced carbon dioxide emissions, causing seawater pH to drop by about 0.1 units since the industrial revolution. This makes it harder for corals to build and maintain their calcium carbonate skeletons, and slows reef growth rates. Some models project that by 2100, ocean chemistry could make reef-building impossible for many species in current form.

Sea Level Rise:
Rising sea levels can outpace some coral species' ability to grow upward, potentially drowning reefs, particularly slower-growing species. This effect is compounded when combined with other stressors reducing coral growth rates.

Increased Storm Intensity:
Climate change is associated with more intense tropical storms and hurricanes, which can physically break coral structures. While reefs can recover from occasional storm damage, increased frequency reduces recovery time between events.

Changing Ocean Currents:
Shifts in ocean circulation patterns affect nutrient distribution and larval dispersal, potentially disrupting the connectivity between reef populations that allows for genetic diversity and recovery after disturbances.

Compounding Effects:
These climate stressors rarely act alone. A reef weakened by acidification is more vulnerable to bleaching; a reef damaged by a storm has reduced capacity to recover from subsequent heat stress. This compounding effect is why scientists increasingly describe coral reefs as facing a survival crisis rather than a single isolated threat.

Projections: Under current emission trajectories, most coral reefs worldwide are projected to experience severe degradation by 2050, with warming of 1.5 degrees Celsius (the lower Paris Agreement target) still expected to cause loss of 70-90% of existing coral reefs.
